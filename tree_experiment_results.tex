Three observations stand out. First, during the initial viewpoints the two
planners are equivalent: both reach \SI{80}{\percent} ROI coverage after
$4$--$5$ views, since the freely visible portion of the fruit cluster can be
captured by any reasonable strategy. The advantage of receding-horizon
planning only materialises where occlusion dominates. GradientNBV plateaus at
\SIrange{90}{93}{\percent} coverage at every orientation -- its local
gradient steps keep the camera near already-observed surfaces and cannot
recover fruit regions hidden behind foliage -- and at one orientation it
never reaches the \SI{90}{\percent} threshold within the 20-view budget.
RH-NBV continues to convert occluded fruit surfaces throughout the run and
attains \SIrange{95}{98}{\percent} coverage at all four orientations,
reaching \SI{90}{\percent} coverage $2.2$ views earlier on average.\\

Second, RH-NBV is not only better on average but also more consistent: its
standard deviation is smaller for every accuracy metric (e.g.\ $\pm 1.4$ vs.\
$\pm 2.8$ percentage points of final coverage), even though the four
orientations expose the planners to visibly different occlusion patterns
(first-view fruit recall ranges from $0.72$ to $0.88$). The receding-horizon
search compensates for unfavourable orientations that trap the purely local
baseline.\\

Third, the accuracy gain is paid for in motion: RH-NBV travels roughly six
times the end-effector path length ($2.50$ vs.\ \SI{0.44}{\metre} on
average). In applications where cycle time or energy dominates, this
trade-off must be weighed; where perception completeness of occluded fruit
is the objective -- as in harvesting -- the additional motion buys the
decisive difference between a plateaued and a near-complete reconstruction.
No planner hyper-parameters were retuned for the plant scenes
(Section~\ref{sec:tree-setup}), so these results also indicate that the proposed
planner generalises across object classes without adjustment.

The blossom level inverts the difficulty profile of the fruit level: the
three blossoms sit in the upper canopy and expose most of their surface to
the viewpoint workspace, so raw coverage saturates for both planners --
\SI{80}{\percent} after about four views, above \SI{97}{\percent} at the end
of the budget, with near-identical convergence speed. The separation lies in
reconstruction fidelity rather than coverage. GradientNBV's recall depends
strongly on where the plant happens to occlude: it ranges from $0.78$ to
$0.96$ over the four orientations ($0.877 \pm 0.067$), because blossom
surfaces facing away from its locally confined vantage points are never
voxelised. RH-NBV holds recall between $0.92$ and $0.95$ at every
orientation ($0.941 \pm 0.011$), lifting $F_1$ to $0.965 \pm 0.009$ against
$0.932 \pm 0.040$; as at the fruit level, the receding-horizon planner is
not only better on average but markedly more consistent across occlusion
patterns. Figure~\ref{fig:tree-blossom-curves} makes the mechanism visible:
the coverage curves are barely distinguishable, whereas the $F_1$ curves
separate after roughly nine views -- the point at which the freely visible
surface is exhausted and further improvement requires actively seeking the
occluded remainder. The price is again motion: $1.54$ vs.\
\SI{0.47}{\metre} of end-effector path, roughly a factor of three.

At the coarsest attention level the picture reverses. The whole plant is
largely self-visible -- occlusion hides individual organs behind foliage,
not the canopy as a whole -- and the local baseline exploits this:
GradientNBV attains $96.6 \pm 0.8\,\%$ coverage and an $F_1$ of $0.992$ at
every orientation while travelling only \SI{0.55}{\metre}. RH-NBV trails on
every aggregate metric, and the deficit is not the product of a single
unlucky run: even with its worst orientation excluded, its mean coverage
($90.1\,\%$) remains below the baseline's worst single orientation
($95.2\,\%$). When most of the target is observable from any reasonable
vantage point, global exploration has little occluded surface left to
convert, while its motion overhead ($2.3\times$ here) remains.\\

Two orientation-specific failure modes widen the gap further. At
$\psi = 180^\circ$ coverage plateaus at $59.5\,\%$: within the first six
views nearly all voxels the planner models as observable have been observed,
its information-gain field flattens, and with no utility gradient left the
camera barely moves for the remaining budget (total path \SI{0.92}{\metre},
the shortest of any RH-NBV run). The resulting model shows the complete
plant silhouette with uniformly sparse leaf detail rather than a single
blind region, and its $F_1$ of $0.992$ confirms that what was reconstructed
is accurate -- the planner stopped looking, not mapping. At
$\psi = 270^\circ$ coverage is normal ($88.7\,\%$) but spuriously occupied
voxels depress precision to $0.79$ (recall $0.993$ is unaffected), pulling
$F_1$ down to $0.880$.\\

Note that final recall is near-saturated ($\approx 0.98$) and
indistinguishable between the planners at this level: with the
\SI{10}{\milli\metre} matching threshold on a densely foliaged plant,
ground-truth points on unseen leaf faces are frequently matched by
reconstructed voxels of nearby observed structure. Coverage, which requires
every ground-truth point to have been directly observed, is therefore the
discriminative quality metric here.\\

Taken together, the three attention levels expose a consistent pattern: the
value of receding-horizon exploration scales with how strongly the target is
occluded. For the heavily self-occluded fruits it converts a plateaued
reconstruction into a near-complete one; for the partially exposed blossoms
it buys reconstruction fidelity at equal coverage; for the largely visible
whole plant the local baseline is faster, cheaper, and more complete.
Neither planner dominates across levels -- the appropriate choice is a
property of the perception task, and specifically of how much of the target
its attention level leaves hidden.

\subsection{Ablation: receding-horizon search vs.\ occlusion bonus}
\label{sec:tree-results-ablation}

RH-NBV differs from the gradient baseline in two separable respects: it
scores globally sampled candidate sequences over a receding horizon, and its
utility augments the shared information-gain objective with an additive
bonus (weight $2.0$) on voxels estimated to be occluded. To attribute the
results of the preceding sections to these two components, we repeat one run
per attention level at the $\psi = 0^\circ$ orientation with the base
utility, i.e.\ the occlusion bonus removed and all other parameters
unchanged. Under the base utility RH-NBV optimises exactly the same
objective as GradientNBV, so this variant is the direct test of the
receding-horizon search itself; comparing it against the full configuration
used throughout Sections~\ref{sec:tree-results-fruit}\,--\,\ref{sec:tree-results-all}
(bonus weight $2.0$) then isolates the contribution of the occlusion bonus.
Table~\ref{tab:tree-ablation} reports both alongside the baseline.

The decomposition assigns each level's outcome to a different component. At
the fruit level the receding-horizon search alone already outperforms the
baseline it shares an objective with -- $94.9$ vs.\ $90.7\,\%$ coverage and
a higher $F_1$ -- so the fruit-level advantage of
Section~\ref{sec:tree-results-fruit} originates in the global candidate
search, not in the modified utility. The occlusion bonus then chiefly buys
convergence speed: it advances the \SI{90}{\percent}-coverage crossing from
view $13$ to view $8$ and lifts the coverage AUC from $72.6$ to $86.7\,\%$,
adding a further $3.1$ points of final coverage.\\

At the blossom level the bonus is behaviourally inert: with the fixed
candidate-sampling seed, both RH-NBV variants select the identical viewpoint
sequence -- per-view path lengths agree to within a millimetre and the same
single motion failure occurs at the same view -- and the residual metric
differences are sensor noise. On a target whose surface is largely exposed,
too few voxels are ever estimated as occluded for the bonus to change any
candidate ranking, which confirms that the bonus does not distort planning
where occlusion is absent.\\

At the whole-plant level the bonus is what keeps RH-NBV competitive at all:
without it the planner inherits the utility-saturation stall of
Section~\ref{sec:tree-results-all} in aggravated form, passing
\SI{80}{\percent} coverage only at the final view (coverage AUC $57.2$ vs.\
$79.7\,\%$ with the bonus). Notably, under the shared objective the local
gradient ascent is clearly superior to the global search here
($96.9$ vs.\ $87.4\,\%$) -- when nearly everything is visible, fine local
refinement progresses steadily while globally sampled candidates barely
differ in utility. The whole-plant deficit of RH-NBV is therefore not an
artefact of an overly aggressive bonus -- removing it widens the gap --
but a property of the level itself. Across the three levels the pattern of
Section~\ref{sec:tree-results-all} sharpens: the receding-horizon search
supplies the advantage under heavy occlusion, the occlusion bonus
accelerates and stabilises it, and neither mechanism pays where the target
is already visible.

the preceding sections -- the same per-level grid, voxel size, matching
threshold, seeds, and viewpoint workspace, and the same driver-level

reachability correction of any kind was applied to their commanded poses.

Three observations emerge. First, the fruit level delivers a result that
strengthens the central claim of this chapter from an unexpected
direction: the \emph{random} sampler outperforms GradientNBV at every
orientation ($96.1 \pm 0.5$ vs.\ $92.9 \pm 2.8\,\%$ coverage) while
matching RH-NBV's convergence speed ($8.5$ views to \SI{90}{\percent}).
Under heavy occlusion, undirected viewpoint diversity alone recovers more
hidden surface than informed local refinement -- the gradient planner's
weakness at this level is not a calibration issue but a structural
limitation of local search. RH-NBV nevertheless leads all four planners on
every fruit-level quality metric, showing that \emph{informed} exploration
still adds a further $1.2$ coverage points and $0.019$ $F_1$ over blind
diversity.\\

Second, PSO illustrates the practical failure mode of
reachability-unaware global optimisation. The viewpoints it reaches are
excellent -- at the whole-plant level its executed views yield the best
$F_1$ of the entire experiment ($0.998$) and near-Gradient coverage
($95.7$ vs.\ $96.6\,\%$) -- but fewer than half of its commanded poses are
executable at any level, and at the fruit level its coverage collapses to
$82.3\,\%$ with the largest variance in the table. Its swarm concentrates
on high-gain regions of the camera-parameter box with no notion of the
arm's kinematic envelope, a constraint the other three planners largely
respect by construction (GradientNBV and Random through local steps around
reachable poses, RH-NBV through its reach model).\\

Third, the whole-plant level separates the two exploratory baselines in an
instructive way: Random plateaus at $84.9\,\%$ -- almost exactly RH-NBV's
$82.4\,\%$ -- while the exploitative planners (GradientNBV, and PSO's
executed views) exceed $95\,\%$. This mirrors the ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:tree-results-ablation} from the opposite side: when the
target is largely visible, \emph{any} diversity-driven strategy pays a
coverage penalty, whether the diversity is random or information-driven.
Taken together with the two-planner results, the four-planner comparison
sharpens the chapter's conclusion into a single spectrum: as target
occlusion increases, the best strategy shifts from local refinement
(GradientNBV) through undirected diversity (Random) to informed
receding-horizon exploration (RH-NBV) -- and the appropriate planner is a
property of the attention level, not of the planner ranking at any single
level.
